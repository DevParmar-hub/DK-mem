\section{Limitations and Ethics}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Scale and seeds.} One seed with greedy decoding; 36 Hindi gold-different and 27 gold-same pairs; wide intervals. Greedy decoding
makes reruns identical, so extra seeds would add nothing and the uncertainty we report is over pairs (Wilson intervals, bootstrap), not over
sampling. Tier~1 (3B backbone, provisional LLM-annotated labels) and Tier~2 (7B judge) are separate experiments and are never pooled. The
threshold $\tau=0.90$ is a pilot value, not a selected operating point, and nothing here shows that the judge is a good choice of judge.

\paragraph{No held-out test set.} All numbers are development results. The Team~B test labels were exposed during verification, and the
24-item Hindi reserve set is not blind: 8 of its 24 items resemble exposed items (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). The independent test set we plan
has not been built.

\paragraph{Constructed data and conventions.} The sentences are templated and constructed, not collected from users, so we make no claim
about how often the failure occurs in natural use. Labels follow four stated conventions, and some are contestable: that a register shift
with the same vocative is one person, and that German du/Sie marks two addressees. The first-version kinship pairs also differ in city or
weekday, a confound that the mirror arm (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}) was built to counter. Class and relation are confounded: kinship pairs are all gold-different and
Hindi register pairs are all gold-same, so a class effect cannot be separated from a relation effect. All 27 gold-same pairs are register or
name variants, so the gate's cost on same-entity \emph{kinship} pairs is untested.

\paragraph{Languages.} The v1.0 and mirror results are Hindi-only (the German pairs appear only in the ``all'' rows). The v2 arm adds
Marathi and Gujarati, but those items have not been reviewed by native speakers, each language contributes only about a sixth of the
pairs, and the judge's behaviour in those languages has not been validated, so their per-language numbers are indicative only.

\paragraph{Lexicon and Marathi review.} The v1 lexicon has 15 Hindi entries and is context-free, so it can mis-tag a word used outside its intended sense:
the Hindi reflexive \emph{apne aap} is tagged as the polite pronoun \emph{aap} (no frozen benchmark text contains this phrase). The same limitation applies more broadly
to kinship terms used as address for non-relatives and to register terms that also mark number. Marathi \emph{tumhi}, for example, is both a polite and a plural ``you''.
No Marathi or Gujarati lexicon entries are part of v1; the Marathi and Gujarati entries added in v2 are unvalidated, and their suffix lists omit some inflected forms and all romanised inflections.
The v2 results also expose coverage gaps in the Hindi entries (periphrases such as ``mother's sister'', a missing spelling, and a possessive phrase tagged as the wrong relative), and the register class
vetoes register shifts that our labels treat as one addressee. The Marathi and Gujarati specifications, distractor turns and proposed lexicon entries
have had only a limited preliminary check, which is neither independent nor native validation.
Repaired items and uncertain labels (for example, kinship same-pairs that rely on an unstated single-relative assumption) need native-speaker confirmation before any future benchmark release.

\paragraph{Ethics.} The benchmark contains no personal data; every sentence is constructed. A kinship lexicon nevertheless encodes particular
family structures, such as separate terms for paternal and maternal relatives, and treats them as identity-bearing, which can embed a normative
view of the family. Kinship words are also used for people who are not relatives (a Gujarati \emph{kaka} may address any older man), so a gate
can veto a correct merge or, if misconfigured, ignore a real distinction. The two error types are not symmetric: a wrongly vetoed merge keeps
two records apart and can be repaired later, while a wrong merge destroys a fact. A deployed system should expose vetoed and unresolved pairs
to the user instead of resolving them silently.
