\paragraph{Hindi data.} The Hindi pairs are templated, constructed sentences and do not reflect real-world frequencies. The 32 kinship
pairs, all gold-different, also differ in city or weekday, so the attribute alone can separate them. Other labels rest on conventions that
real Hindi usage need not follow: a shared name means one person, although two people can share a name; a \emph{tum}/\emph{aap} or
\emph{tu}/\emph{tum} shift with the same vocative means one addressee, although such switches read as awkward in several pairs and the
turns address the relative directly; and ``school wali'' and ``gym wali'' Neha are two people, although one person can be both and the
source pair may describe two events involving one person. A
co-author reviewed all \ReviewHiPairsTotal{} pairs against these conventions with the gold labels visible, so the review is neither
independent nor blind: \ReviewHiPairsCorrect{} correct, \ReviewHiPairsAmbiguous{} ambiguous (\emph{tu}/\emph{tum}),
\ReviewHiPairsWrong{} wrong, 14 awkward (mostly formal weekday names) and four qualifier pairs misclassified as \emph{name\_variant}.
Independent, blind native-speaker review is still needed.
