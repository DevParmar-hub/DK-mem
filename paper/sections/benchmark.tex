\section{Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\paragraph{Construction.} We evaluate on a controlled injection benchmark (Tier~2, v1.0). Each of 69 source pairs becomes two constructed
user sentences that carry the pair's distinction marker (a kinship term, a name in Roman vs.\ Devanagari script, or a register pronoun)
and state different attribute values (city, weekday or birthday month). Both are injected into one synthetic conversation of six weekly
sessions, each with four alternating user/assistant turns, 1, 3 or 5 sessions apart (23 pairs each). The other ten user turns come from a
fixed bank of hand-authored neutral distractors, validated to contain no injected name, lexicon term, register pronoun, family term or
answer value; assistant turns are generic acknowledgements, and no turn is model-generated. After the last session, 111 questions are
asked: one per member of a gold-different pair, with the other member's value as a forbidden answer, and one per gold-same pair that needs
both facts; scoring is deterministic. The system under test sees only the conversations. The evaluated arm has 63 Hindi pairs (36
gold-different, 27 gold-same) and 6 German gold-different register pairs (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}). All sentences are constructed from
templates; we make no claim about how often these distinctions occur in natural conversation.

\paragraph{Labelling conventions.} Gold labels come by construction from the provisional, LLM-annotated Tier~1 labels. Since identity is
underdetermined in short constructed sentences, the labels follow four stated conventions, not facts about the world: (1) the same name
without a distinguishing qualifier is the same person; (2) a register shift (\emph{tu}/\emph{tum}/\emph{aap}) with the same vocative,
e.g.\ ``Papa'', is the same person; (3) gold-same kinship pairs force identity with a shared name or apposition (no such pair is in v1.0);
(4) a contextual qualifier (``school wali Neha'' vs.\ ``gym wali Neha'') overrides name identity. Four construction effects remain. The
32 Hindi kinship pairs also differ in city or weekday, so the attribute alone separates them. Class is confounded with relation: Hindi
kinship pairs are all gold-different, Hindi register pairs all gold-same, German register pairs all gold-different. Register turns address
the relative directly inside a user--assistant chat (``Papa, tum Delhi ja rahe ho''), which is less natural than reported speech but keeps
the pronoun. The 20 Hindi gold-same name-variant turns with a Devanagari name are the only Devanagari-script turns.

\paragraph{Mirror arm.} In v1.0 the attribute pattern predicts the label: gold-different pairs state one attribute with two values (e.g.\
two cities), gold-same pairs two different attributes (e.g.\ a birthday month and a city). The mirror arm v1.1m removes this shortcut by
replacing only member~$b$'s sentence in each of the 69 conversations: gold-different pairs now state a different attribute, gold-same
pairs the same attribute with a conflicting value. Labels, markers, session positions and distractors are unchanged, so the pair counts
are identical (63 Hindi, 6 German); together with v1.0, relation is balanced over the attribute pattern in every class $\times$ relation
stratum. The mirror arm has no questions.

\paragraph{Marathi and Gujarati extension.} Team B's draft v2 package adds Marathi and Gujarati items and drops German. Its repaired
development version v2.1 has 126 pair specifications (Hindi 81, Marathi 23, Gujarati 22). Eighteen were repaired to satisfy the
question--answer contract (e.g.\ both members stated the same city, or the question contained its own answer), with relation, class,
language and ids unchanged; 13 of these change Marathi or Gujarati text without prior native verification. New banks of 30 hand-authored
distractor user turns were written for each language. No system was run on v2.1, and we report no Marathi or Gujarati result.

\paragraph{In-team review.} A native Hindi speaker, a co-author, reviewed the Hindi items with the gold labels visible, so the review is
in-team and not independent. Each Hindi pair evaluated in Sec.~\ref{sec:results} was judged on label correctness under
the conventions above, naturalness and class. Of the \ReviewHiPairsTotal{} Hindi pairs, \ReviewHiPairsCorrect{} labels were judged
correct, \ReviewHiPairsWrong{} wrong and \ReviewHiPairsAmbiguous{} ambiguous: the only pair that shifts between \emph{tu} and \emph{tum}
rather than \emph{tum} and \emph{aap} (addressed to ``Maa''), which Team B's audit also flagged. The four qualifier pairs were marked as
misclassified, because the qualifier, not the name form, carries the distinction. For naturalness, 14 pairs were judged awkward but
plausible and none implausible; the comments cite formal weekday names (e.g.\ \emph{mangalvar}) in casual Hinglish and \emph{tum}/\emph{aap}
switches towards the same addressee. No label was edited: pairs marked wrong or ambiguous are excluded only in a sensitivity analysis
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results}), and the primary numbers use all pairs. The Marathi and Gujarati extension and the six German pairs have not
been reviewed by native speakers.

\paragraph{Splits and exposure.} Every number in this paper is a development result. The 63 Hindi v1.0 pairs were used throughout
development and are in the development split of Team B's v2 package. That package's 30-pair test split of new items had its sentences
and gold labels listed in a prompt sent to an LLM verifier; we treat it as exposed, moved it to development data in v2.1, and do not use
it as a test set. A 24-item Hindi reserve set was written after that exposure and partly derives from exposed items, so it is not blind
either. No held-out test set exists yet.
